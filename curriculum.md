## Phase 1: Fundamentals (3-4 months, ~110-140 hours)

**Focus: Aerodynamics theory and foundational structures**

**Books:**
- "Introduction to Flight" (Anderson) - Chapters 1-11
- "Mechanics of Materials" (Beer/Johnston) - Chapters 1-8

**What to skip:**

- Skim the history sections (read for interest, don't study)
- Skip pilot perspective sections (you live this daily)

**What to emphasize:**
- Airfoil theory (Chapter 4) - calculate Cl, Cd for NACA airfoils
- Finite wing theory (Chapter 5) - induced drag, aspect ratio effects
- Compressible flow basics (Chapter 8-9) - Mach effects even at low speed
- Stress/strain fundamentals - bending, torsion, combined loading
- Beam theory and deflection calculations

**Deliverables:**
- Calculate lift/drag polar for 3 candidate airfoils
- Analyze why Super Decathlon uses semi-symmetric airfoil vs symmetric
- Design a simple beam (spar) for given bending moment
- **Milestone:** Choose your baseline airfoil and justify it

**Time breakdown:**
- Anderson: 60 hours (you'll move fast, you understand the physics)
- Beer/Johnston: 50 hours (math is natural for you)
- Problem sets: Critical - do 50% of end-of-chapter problems

## Critical Success Factors

**1. Work problems religiously**
Your math background is an advantage, but aircraft design is applied math. Do 50% of textbook problems minimum. The learning happens in struggling through calculations.

**2. Use software tools early**
- **XFLR5** (free): Airfoil and wing analysis, stability derivatives
- **OpenVSP** (free): Aircraft geometry and configuration
- **Python/MATLAB**: Write scripts for repetitive calculations
- You're a software architect - automate everything you can

**3. Study real aircraft obsessively**
- Get Super Decathlon drawings (American Champion, or study similar aircraft)
- Join EAA, attend airshows, talk to builders
- Measure everything you can on actual aircraft
- Type certificates and flight manuals are free online

**4. Validate constantly**
Compare your calculations to known aircraft:
- Does your wing loading match similar aircraft?
- Is your power-to-weight reasonable?
- Are your structural member sizes in the ballpark?
- If you're way off, figure out why

**5. Your pilot experience is gold**
When you calculate that the stick force should be 35 lbs for 4g, you can mentally reference what that actually feels like. When Raymer says "maneuvering speed around 1.7 Vs", you know exactly what he means. Use this constantly.

**6. Think in systems**
Your software architecture mindset applies perfectly. Aircraft design is:
- Interconnected subsystems
- Constraint satisfaction
- Optimization under trade-offs
- Iteration and refinement

Everything affects everything: change wing area → changes weight → changes engine size → changes cost → changes fuel capacity → changes CG range. Your ability to think systematically will accelerate your learning.
